Roll of Honour: Those Who Served

A permanent record of Brampton people commemorated at Brampton Old Church, St Martin’s Church and on local wartime memorials, honouring military, nursing and support service.

Photo: Brampton Old Church Cemetery by Alan Sawyer, CC BY-NC-ND 2.0

This Roll of Honour remembers men and women whose service is commemorated at Brampton Old Church and its churchyard, St Martin’s Church and churchyard in the town centre, and Brampton’s New Cemetery. It honours those who died during service, veterans who returned home, and people who supported wartime medical care and relief. Some are buried here; others are remembered locally while their graves or official memorials lie elsewhere.

Entries are arranged by conflict or period of service. Each card gives a short account; select Read full biography for the fuller story and the sources used. Where one person served in more than one conflict, separate cards are linked and open the same full biography covering all their service. Within each period, recorded ranks run from senior to junior; entries without a recorded rank and civilian support roles follow. A memorial reference identifies an inscription, not necessarily a burial.

Biographies draw on local accounts and the individual records linked below each entry. Memorial wording, casualty records and later research sometimes differ; those differences are retained where they cannot be securely resolved. A linked record described as a possible identification is a research lead, not a confirmed service history.

Additions or corrections are welcome. Please contact Brittany here.

A memorial in St Martin's Church remembers service at the siege of Seringapatam.

St Martin's ChurchN17

Captain Mark Deane Buckeridge

Captain, 74th Regiment of Foot; siege of Seringapatam, 1799. Died after the siege; sources give his age as 27 or 29.

Mark Deane Buckeridge of Brampton matriculated at St John’s College, Oxford, in 1793 and later served with the 74th Regiment at Seringapatam. He survived the siege but died of fever afterwards. Sources disagree over his age, giving twenty-seven or twenty-nine.

Brampton memorial location: Old Churchyard · G16

Burial / commemoration: Brampton Old Churchyard; family memorial G16.

Charles Burrough served in the Royal Navy during the wars with Revolutionary France. At the Battle of Camperdown on 11 October 1797 he was First Lieutenant of the 74-gun HMS Russell. William James’s Naval History independently lists him among the first lieutenants at the battle, spelling his name Charles Burroughs and the ship Russel. The Brampton memorial uses Burrough and Russell.

James records that the first lieutenants of the ships present received a step in rank after the victory. This provides a historical context for the later rank of Master and Commander on Burrough’s memorial, although an individual promotion order and subsequent commands have not been established. His first-lieutenant appointment at Camperdown is kept distinct from that later rank.

He died on 13 July 1810, aged fifty-seven. The Old Churchyard memorial also remembers Thomas, the son of Charles and Ann Burrough, who died on 30 November 1808, aged five. It preserves both his naval service and a loss within his family.

St Martin's ChurchN17

Captain Mark Deane Buckeridge

Captain, 74th Regiment of Foot; siege of Seringapatam, 1799. Died after the siege; sources give his age as 27 or 29.

Brampton memorial location: St Martin's Church · N17

Burial / commemoration: The Gentleman’s Magazine account records burial at Seringapatam, India. The Brampton memorial is inside St Martin's Church, N17.

Mark Deane Buckeridge was a son of Henry Buckeridge of Brampton. Joseph Foster’s Alumni Oxonienses records his matriculation at St John’s College, Oxford, on 27 June 1793, aged twenty-one. The full name and Brampton connection provide a useful independent record of his life before the Indian campaign.

The account from The Gentleman’s Magazine (1800), reproduced in the Brampton memorial transcription, describes service with the 12th Regiment in Holland and Flanders, a knee injury and recurring ill health. It places his exchange into the 74th Regiment early in 1799. His subsequent service at the siege of Seringapatam is also recorded by the Imperial War Museums' transcription of the tablet in St Martin’s Church.

In a letter reproduced in that magazine account, Buckeridge described the hardships of trench duty and the assault on 4 May. He survived the fighting but died after another illness, described on the tablet as fever. The account records burial at Seringapatam; George Elers’s memoirs also recall a Captain Buckeridge dying in the hospital established in the Lal Bagh palace.

The sources differ over his age. The Cumbria Family History Society transcription gives twenty-seven, whereas the IWM transcription reads twenty-nine. The Oxford entry’s age of twenty-one in 1793 is consistent with an age of about twenty-seven in 1799.

Local historical material also associates him with the house on Cross Street which had been occupied by Bonnie Prince Charlie during his stay in Brampton. His memorial in St Martin’s is a local commemoration, not a record of burial in the town.

As a young man Carrick travelled to South Africa and joined the East Griqualand Mounted Rifles, serving during the final year of the Second Boer War, which ended in May 1902. He was also recorded as a farmer’s pupil in 1902. Following the conflict he pursued farming in Canada. His elder brother, John William Preston D. Carrick, had emigrated in 1896 and settled at Pine Lake, Alberta. In 1908 Lawrence and his youngest sister, Eleanor Rosalind, travelled to Canada to join him. Eleanor later married Charles Atter and remained in Alberta.

Carrick continued his association with mounted service in Canada, joining the Canadian Light Horse and serving with the Royal North-West Mounted Police. After the outbreak of the First World War he enlisted on 23 September 1914, joining the 19th Alberta Dragoons under service number 1932. He travelled to Europe with the First Canadian Contingent.

His enlistment papers describe him as five feet five and a half inches tall, with fair hair and a medium complexion. A distinctive detail was a tattoo of a tiger’s head on his right forearm. He later served as a lieutenant in the 49th Battalion, Canadian Infantry (Alberta Regiment), which was fighting on the Somme by September 1916.

On 15 September 1916, during the fighting around Courcelette, Carrick was shot through the head by a sniper at approximately eight o’clock in the morning. Trinity School’s biographical account records him among 128 men of the Alberta Regiment who died that day. The Cumberland News reported that he had been killed only twenty yards from the German front line, and a letter from a fellow officer remembered him as a capable, genial and much-liked companion.

Carrick has no known identified grave and is named on the Canadian National Vimy Memorial in France. In Brampton he is also remembered on the Carrick family memorial in the Old Churchyard (E122), which records his death at Courcelette on 15 September 1916; on the First World War tablet inside St Martin’s Church (N5); and on a separate brass plaque there (N8).

The school biography contains a small but important internal discrepancy: its heading correctly gives 15 September 1916, while one sentence in the narrative prints 1915. The family commemoration and casualty record agree on 1916. His number 1932 belongs to his earlier enlisted service; the rank by which he is remembered at his death is lieutenant.

The general description ‘killed in a flying accident’ does not mean that he died at the instant of impact.

He was twenty-three and is buried at Victoria (Royal Oak) Burial Park, Section D, Plot 21, Grave 13. His mother visited his grave in 1950. The Brampton memorial remembers an airman whose death occurred in overseas training, not on an operational flight over enemy territory.

Sister Constance Mary Nichol

Sister, Queen Alexandra’s Imperial Military Nursing Service Reserve, service no. 336877 · died 15 August 1945, aged 22

Brampton memorial location: St Martin’s Church · N9 · Second World War memorial

Burial / commemoration: Yaba Cemetery, Lagos · Plot 4, Row G, Grave 7

Constance Mary Nichol was the daughter of William and Ethel Nichol of Brampton. She served as a sister in Queen Alexandra’s Imperial Military Nursing Service Reserve, under number 336877, and is remembered on the town’s tablet as Sister C. M. Nichol.

The recorded casualty information places her with 44 West African General Hospital. She died on 15 August 1945, aged twenty-two, and is recorded as buried in Yaba Cemetery, Lagos, Nigeria, Plot 4, Row G, Grave 7.

Her death came on the date remembered as VJ Day. The date alone does not explain the circumstances: no reliable cause of death has been established here. A precise date for her nursing appointment has not been established from an individual London Gazette notice.

Identity unresolved

Pilot Officer W. O. Simpson

Pilot Officer, Royal Air Force

Brampton memorial location: St Martin’s Church · N9 · Second World War memorial

Burial / commemoration: Not securely established for the man named on the Brampton plaque.

The Second World War plaque in St Martin’s Church records W. O. Simpson, Pilot Officer, Royal Air Force. Direct inspection confirms the second initial as O. War Memorials Online gives W. E. Simpson; its transcription does not match the plaque.

A possible match is Pilot Officer William Ogilvie Simpson, service number 147597, Royal Air Force Volunteer Reserve. Trinity School’s memorial research records his birth at Gilcrux on 1 November 1917 and attendance at Creighton School from 1929 to 1933. His parents were Adam Ogilvie and Mary Lizzie Simpson of Carlisle; his wife was Doreen Simpson of Botcherby, Carlisle.

The school account gives his death as 3 March 1944, aged twenty-six, and burial in Bari War Cemetery, Italy. It reports a motor-transport accident near Barletta, citing Jon Heyworth’s Unaccounted Project; the accident details have not been independently verified here.

The initials, rank, service and Cumberland background make him a plausible candidate. However, the sources consulted do not explicitly connect William Ogilvie Simpson with Brampton or this plaque. These personal and service details remain a research lead, not an established identification.

Sources

  • St Martin’s Church, Brampton, Second World War memorial (N9): direct inspection of the plaque confirms W. O. Simpson.
  • War Memorials Online, Brampton Second World War memorial (WMO/176876); the online transcription incorrectly gives W. E. Simpson.
  • Trinity School War Memorial: William O. Simpson (William Ogilvie Simpson), a possible identification; the biography cites school, census and casualty records, and Jon Heyworth’s Unaccounted Project.

Private Norman Skelton

Private, 1st (Airborne) Battalion Border Regiment · died 23 September 1944, aged 19

Brampton memorial location: St Martin’s Church · N9 · Second World War memorial

Burial / commemoration: Arnhem Oosterbeek War Cemetery

Norman Skelton was the son of Robert and Mrs Skelton of Brampton. ParaData’s account records that he enlisted through the General Service Corps, volunteered for airborne service and completed glider training before joining the 1st (Airborne) Battalion Border Regiment.

He fought during Operation Market Garden in September 1944, was wounded and died of his wounds on 23 September, aged nineteen. His service with an airlanding battalion is not described as a parachute drop: the individual account specifies glider training.

He was first buried behind the Paasberg school at Oosterbeek. ParaData gives 10 September 1945 as the date of his reinterment in Arnhem Oosterbeek War Cemetery. That later date relates to the movement of his remains, not to his death. His name is also preserved on Brampton’s Second World War memorial.

Private John Wightman

Private, 1st (Airborne) Battalion Border Regiment, service no. 3604115 · died 25 September 1944, aged 29

Brampton memorial location: St Martin’s Church · N9 · Second World War memorial

Burial / commemoration: Arnhem Oosterbeek War Cemetery

John Wightman was born in Dumfriesshire and lived in Cumberland. He enlisted in the Border Regiment, volunteered for airborne service and completed glider training before serving with the 1st (Airborne) Battalion Border Regiment. His service number was 3604115.

He fought in Operation Market Garden and was killed on 25 September 1944, aged twenty-nine. His name is remembered in Brampton alongside Norman Skelton, another member of the battalion who died during the same operation.

ParaData records that his original field burial location was not known. Civilians from Oosterbeek subsequently brought his remains to Arnhem Oosterbeek War Cemetery. His grave there is the final recorded resting place; the exact original burial site is not reconstructed from the later commemoration.
