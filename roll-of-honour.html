Roll of Honour: Those Who Served

A permanent record of Brampton people commemorated at Brampton Old Church, St Martin’s Church and on local wartime memorials, honouring military, nursing and support service.

Photo: Brampton Old Church Cemetery by Alan Sawyer, CC BY-NC-ND 2.0

This Roll of Honour remembers men and women whose service is commemorated at Brampton Old Church and its churchyard, St Martin’s Church and churchyard in the town centre, and Brampton’s New Cemetery. It honours those who died during service, veterans who returned home, and people who supported wartime medical care and relief. Some are buried here; others are remembered locally while their graves or official memorials lie elsewhere.

Entries are arranged by conflict or period of service. Each card gives a short account; select Read full biography for the fuller story and the sources used. Where one person served in more than one conflict, separate profiles are linked so that their service can be followed across the page. Within each period, recorded ranks run from senior to junior; entries without a recorded rank and civilian support roles follow. A memorial reference identifies an inscription, not necessarily a burial.

William John Lawson was born in 1861 and trained as an engineer. Ill health brought him back to England in 1900. After a period at Rothesay, he and his wife Mary Elizabeth settled at Westwood, Brampton. They offered the house and grounds for a cottage hospital in 1920 as part of the town's war memorial scheme. Lawson also served as a churchwarden and justice of the peace. As a young man, Carrick travelled to South Africa and joined the East Griqualand Mounted Rifles, serving during the final year of the Second Boer War. Following the conflict, he pursued farming in Canada. His elder brother, John William Preston D. Carrick, had emigrated in 1896 and settled at Pine Lake, Alberta. In 1908 Lawrence and his youngest sister, Eleanor Rosalind, travelled to Canada to join him. Eleanor later married Charles Atter and made her home in Alberta.

Carrick continued his association with mounted service in Canada, joining the Canadian Light Horse and serving with the Royal North-West Mounted Police. When the First World War began in August 1914, he volunteered for military service. He enlisted on 23 September 1914, joining the 19th Alberta Dragoons under service number 1932. He travelled to Europe with the First Canadian Contingent.

His enlistment papers recorded him as five feet five and a half inches tall, with fair hair and a medium complexion. One particularly distinctive detail was a tattoo of a tiger's head on his right forearm.

Carrick subsequently served as a lieutenant with the 49th Battalion, Canadian Infantry (Alberta Regiment). By September 1916, the battalion was taking part in the fighting on the Somme in northern France.

Second Lieutenant Arthur Hetherington

Second Lieutenant, 9th Battalion King’s Own Yorkshire Light Infantry · died 21/22 March 1918, aged 29

Brampton memorial location: Old Churchyard · B47

Burial / commemoration: His body could not be recovered; no burial location has been established.

Arthur Hetherington was born on 20 August 1888, the youngest of ten children of Samuel Hetherington and Annie Barnfather. His father had worked as a weaver and later as a rural postman or manager and as a boot and shoe dealer. By the 1911 census the family was living at Shields House.

Arthur trained as a teacher. He was a pupil teacher at Carlisle Grammar School from 1905 to 1907, later trained at Dundee and became an assistant master at Haverton Hill School. He enlisted on 9 December 1915, was mobilised in February 1916 and went to France on 15 June 1916. After promotion to lance-corporal he was sent to officer training in Bristol and was commissioned into the 9th Battalion King’s Own Yorkshire Light Infantry in September 1917.

During the great German offensive in March 1918, Hetherington and No. 1 Platoon were caught by German machine-gun fire. His commanding officer told the family that the troops had been forced to withdraw and could not recover his body. His memorial gives 22 March 1918 as the date of death, while contemporary newspaper reporting gives 21 March.

A surviving letter to the War Office was written by his fiancée, Miss Whitehurst, who had heard nothing from Arthur since 13 March and did not know his relatives well enough to obtain news through them. His estate was valued at £200 18s. Two of his cousins, Isaac Hetherington and Cyril Dudley Hetherington, are also commemorated by Brampton.

Old Churchyard E122

Lieutenant Lawrence Stanley Carrick

East Griqualand Mounted Rifles · Second Boer War (1901–1902)

Brampton memorial location: Old Churchyard · E122 (family memorial)

Burial / commemoration: No known grave; commemorated on the Vimy Memorial, France

Lawrence Stanley Carrick was born on 26 June 1882, the youngest of eight children of John Carrick and his wife Catharine Elizabeth, of The Grange, Brampton. His father and grandfather were solicitors, and the family enjoyed a comfortable position in the town. Lawrence's father died when he was eight years old. He attended Carlisle Grammar School between 1892 and 1896 before continuing his education at Rugby School.

Although recorded as a farmer's pupil in 1902, Carrick was drawn towards military service and travelled to South Africa, where he joined the East Griqualand Mounted Rifles. He served with this mounted force during the final year of the Second Boer War, which ended in May 1902.

Following the war, Carrick turned his attention to farming in Canada. His elder brother, John William Preston D. Carrick, had emigrated in 1896 and settled at Pine Lake, Alberta. In 1908 Lawrence and his youngest sister, Eleanor Rosalind, travelled to Canada to join him. Eleanor subsequently married Charles Atter and made her home in Alberta.

Carrick's interest in military and mounted service continued in Canada. He joined the Canadian Light Horse and also served with the Royal North-West Mounted Police. When the First World War began in 1914, he enlisted on 23 September and joined the 19th Alberta Dragoons, travelling to Europe with the first Canadian Contingent.

His enlistment papers provide a distinctive description of him. He stood five feet five and a half inches tall, with fair hair and a medium complexion. He also had a tattoo of a tiger's head on his right forearm.

Carrick subsequently served as a lieutenant in the 49th Battalion, Canadian Infantry (Alberta Regiment). On 15 September 1916, during the fighting on the Somme near Courcelette, he was shot through the head by a sniper at approximately eight o'clock in the morning. Trinity School's biographical account records him as one of 128 members of the Alberta Regiment who died that day.

His obituary in the Cumberland News described how he had been killed only twenty yards from the German front line. A brother officer remembered him as a capable and much-liked officer whose companionship was valued by those who served alongside him.

Carrick's body was not recovered and identified for burial. His name is commemorated on the Canadian National Vimy Memorial in France. He is also remembered in Brampton, including on the family memorial at Old Churchyard reference E122.
