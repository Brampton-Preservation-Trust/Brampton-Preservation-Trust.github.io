The History of Brampton Old Church

A remarkable Cumbrian site where the remains of a Roman fort, a medieval parish church and centuries of local memory occupy the same ground.

Brampton Old Church is the surviving chancel of the medieval Church of St Martin. It stands within the northern half of a late first- or early second-century Roman fort on the Stanegate, surrounded by a historic burial ground and cared for today by Brampton Preservation Trust.

I
Before the church

A Roman fort on the Stanegate

The first proven use of the site was military. A fort was built on a spur above the River Irthing beside the Stanegate, the Roman road linking Corbridge and Carlisle. It was large enough to accommodate an auxiliary unit of around 500 men.

Limited excavation by F. G. Simpson and I. A. Richmond in 1935 identified the defences, the south gateway, granaries, a headquarters building, a barrack block and other structures. Carefully sealed post holes suggest that the fort was deliberately dismantled, probably after Hadrian's Wall and its associated forts became operational.

A site beneath the surface

Much of the fort survives as buried archaeology. The churchyard and the surviving church occupy its northern half.

II
Church of St Martin

The medieval parish church

The earliest documentary evidence for Brampton's church dates to 1169, when Osbertus, “persona de Brampton”, witnessed the dedication deed of Lanercost Priory. The church later served Brampton's medieval parish and was enlarged and altered over many generations.

Several medieval grave covers survive at the site. Among them is the cross-slab of Richard de Caldecotes, vicar of Brampton between 1334 and 1346, carved with a chalice, a book and a Latin inscription. A fortified tower was added at the west end during the fourteenth century, reflecting the insecurity of the Border region.

An early Christian tradition

Local tradition places Christian worship on the abandoned fort at a much earlier date. The dedication to St Martin and the old oval churchyard have been offered as clues, but archaeological evidence would be needed to confirm the tradition.

III
Demolition and adaptation

Why only the chancel survives

By the eighteenth century the old parish church stood well outside the growing town and was in poor condition. A chapel in Brampton town centre had become the more convenient place for worship. Permission to dismantle the old church was granted in 1787, and by 1789 the nave and west tower had been demolished.

The chancel remained to serve the burial ground as a mortuary chapel. The churchyard continued in use and was extended in 1861 and 1889. In 1861 the surviving building was itself extended at the east end and given a porch at the west, helping to create the form seen today.

IV
Protection and care

From redundancy to preservation

The Roman fort and medieval church site became a Scheduled Monument in 1938. The churchyard closed during the 1960s, and the surviving chancel was declared redundant by the Church of England in 1978.

Brampton Preservation Trust was founded in 1981 by local people concerned about the loss of historic buildings and the character of the town. The Trust acquired Brampton Old Church in 1988 and oversaw restoration work. It continues to maintain the building, encourage public access and share the site's history.
